# Anchor Bibliography

A curated starting bibliography for the evidence obligations in `references/evidence-informed-design.md` and `references/evidence-source-protocol.md`. Use it when a consequential recommendation needs a citable source: start here before searching, and record the chosen source in `assets/source-register.md` with the population and limits it actually covers.

Last verified: 2026-08-06.

Rules of use:

- These entries were verified as of the date above. Before citing one to faculty or in a course document, confirm the bibliographic details and check for later editions, corrections, or retractions. When an entry is re-verified against publisher records, add its DOI to the entry — a DOI is the strongest protection this file can offer against misattribution, and its absence means the entry has only been checked by citation details.
- An entry here does not discharge the fit check. Population, discipline, prior knowledge, modality, and implementation conditions still decide whether a finding transfers; apply `references/evidence-source-protocol.md` §2.
- Do not invent additions. If a needed source is not listed and cannot be verified from the original, follow the search protocol rather than citing from memory — a fabricated or misattributed citation is a Blocker in any faculty-facing artifact.
- The stated support uses this skill's strength vocabulary: strong, promising, or context-dependent, always for a stated scope.

## Active learning and its evidence base

- **Freeman, S., Eddy, S. L., McDonough, M., Smith, M. K., Okoroafor, N., Jordt, H., & Wenderoth, M. P. (2014).** Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 111*(23), 8410–8415. — Supports: strong meta-analytic evidence that active learning improves exam performance and reduces failure rates in undergraduate STEM. Limits: compares broad categories, not specific formats; says nothing about which active format fits a given outcome.
- **Theobald, E. J., et al. (2020).** Active learning narrows achievement gaps for underrepresented students in undergraduate science, technology, engineering, and math. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 117*(12), 6476–6483. — Supports: strong evidence that active learning disproportionately benefits underrepresented and low-income students, with larger effects at higher implementation intensity. Limits: intensity, not format, is the moderator examined.
- **Prince, M. (2004).** Does active learning work? A review of the research. *Journal of Engineering Education, 93*(3), 223–231. — Supports: an engineering-audience review distinguishing active, collaborative, cooperative, and problem-based learning and their differing evidence. Limits: a 2004 snapshot; use with the later meta-analyses above.
- **Prince, M. J., & Felder, R. M. (2006).** Inductive teaching and learning methods: Definitions, comparisons, and research bases. *Journal of Engineering Education, 95*(2), 123–138. — Supports: the definitional crosswalk among inquiry, problem-based, project-based, case-based, and discovery formats used in `references/framework-crosswalk.md` §4. Limits: definitional and comparative, not a meta-analysis.
- **Deslauriers, L., McCarty, L. S., Miller, K., Callaghan, K., & Kestin, G. (2019).** Measuring actual learning versus feeling of learning in response to being actively engaged in the classroom. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 116*(39), 19251–19257. — Supports: strong evidence that students learn more from active instruction while *feeling* they learn less — the basis for telling students why harder-feeling work is working. Limits: introductory physics at one institution; the perception–performance gap, not the pedagogy, is the finding to transfer.
- **Chi, M. T. H., & Wylie, R. (2014).** The ICAP framework: Linking cognitive engagement to active learning outcomes. *Educational Psychologist, 49*(4), 219–243. — Supports: the mechanism ordering behind "reject an engaging activity when students can complete it without the intended reasoning": interactive > constructive > active > passive engagement. Limits: a framework synthesis; individual comparisons vary.

## General syntheses

- **Ambrose, S. A., Bridges, M. W., DiPietro, M., Lovett, M. C., & Norman, M. K. (2010).** *How Learning Works: Seven Research-Based Principles for Smart Teaching.* Jossey-Bass. — Supports: accessible faculty-facing synthesis of prior knowledge, organization, motivation, mastery, practice and feedback, climate, and self-directed learning. Limits: principles-level; cite the underlying literature for consequential claims.
- **National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2018).** *How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures.* National Academies Press. — Supports: consensus-report authority for learning mechanisms, culture and context effects, and technology. Limits: broad scope; chapter-level citation recommended.
- **National Research Council (2012).** *Discipline-Based Education Research: Understanding and Improving Learning in Undergraduate Science and Engineering.* National Academies Press. — Supports: the case for discipline-based (rather than generic) education research, including misconception and concept-inventory traditions. Limits: field survey as of 2012.
- **Felder, R. M., & Brent, R. (2016; 2nd ed. 2024).** *Teaching and Learning STEM: A Practical Guide.* Jossey-Bass. — Supports: the standard practice-oriented STEM course-design handbook; closest published analogue to this skill's workflow. Limits: practice guidance; verify individual claims against primary sources when stakes are high.
- **Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013).** Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14*(1), 4–58. — Supports: comparative utility ratings across ten learning techniques; strong for practice testing and distributed practice, low for rereading and highlighting. Limits: utility ratings weigh generality, not just effect size.

## Retrieval, spacing, and interleaving

- **Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006).** Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science, 17*(3), 249–255. — Supports: strong evidence that retrieval practice outperforms restudy for retention. Limits: laboratory prose materials; classroom transfer is well replicated but effect sizes vary.
- **Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006).** Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin, 132*(3), 354–380. — Supports: strong quantitative basis for spacing, including gap-to-retention-interval dependence used in the curriculum-map scheduling guidance. Limits: verbal recall tasks; complex-skill spacing is promising rather than settled.
- **Rohrer, D., & Taylor, K. (2007).** The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning. *Instructional Science, 35*, 481–498. — Supports: interleaving improves later performance in mathematics problem classification and solution while depressing practice performance. Limits: mathematics procedures; interleave only confusable alternatives.
- **Soderstrom, N. C., & Bjork, R. A. (2015).** Learning versus performance: An integrative review. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 10*(2), 176–199. — Supports: the load-bearing distinction that in-session performance is an unreliable index of learning — the warrant for the performance-cost warnings in `references/evidence-informed-design.md` §5. Limits: a review; direction is robust, magnitudes vary.

## Examples, self-explanation, and cognitive load

- **Sweller, J., van Merriënboer, J. J. G., & Paas, F. (2019).** Cognitive architecture and instructional design: 20 years later. *Educational Psychology Review, 31*, 261–292. — Supports: current statement of cognitive load theory, worked-example and expertise-reversal effects. Limits: strongest for well-structured domains and novices; effects reverse with expertise.
- **Renkl, A. (2014).** Toward an instructionally oriented theory of example-based learning. *Cognitive Science, 38*(1), 1–37. — Supports: worked examples with fading and self-explanation prompts. Limits: gains depend on prompting quality and learner prior knowledge.
- **Chi, M. T. H., de Leeuw, N., Chiu, M.-H., & LaVancher, C. (1994).** Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding. *Cognitive Science, 18*(3), 439–477. — Supports: prompted self-explanation improving conceptual understanding. Limits: small-sample foundational study; cite alongside later replications for consequential use.
- **Mayer, R. E. (2021).** *Multimedia Learning* (3rd ed.). Cambridge University Press. — Supports: evidence-based multimedia principles (coherence, signaling, contiguity, segmenting) behind the load and visual-design checks. Limits: mostly short lessons with novice learners.

## Misconceptions, concept inventories, and bottlenecks

- **Hestenes, D., Wells, M., & Swackhamer, G. (1992).** Force Concept Inventory. *The Physics Teacher, 30*(3), 141–158. — Supports: the original validated concept inventory and the model for the instrument-before-homemade-diagnostic rule. Limits: Newtonian mechanics; validated for its population and use, per `references/evidence-informed-design.md` §3.
- **Meyer, J. H. F., & Land, R. (2005).** Threshold concepts and troublesome knowledge (2): Epistemological considerations and a conceptual framework for teaching and learning. *Higher Education, 49*, 373–388. — Supports: the threshold-concept construct used in the diagnosis guidance — transformative, integrative, troublesome bottlenecks that resist single-cycle conceptual change. Limits: a conceptual framework, not an effect-size literature; identification of thresholds is discipline work.
- **Middendorf, J., & Pace, D. (2004).** Decoding the disciplines: A model for helping students learn disciplinary ways of thinking. *New Directions for Teaching and Learning, 2004*(98), 1–12. — Supports: the interview method for surfacing expert blind spots when instructors misestimate where students fail. Limits: a process model; evidence is practice-based.

## Motivation, self-efficacy, and belonging

- **Bandura, A. (1997).** *Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control.* W. H. Freeman. — Supports: the theoretical basis for mastery experiences, attributional feedback, and the early-success sequencing rule. Limits: theory plus broad evidence; classroom operationalizations vary.
- **Eccles, J. S., & Wigfield, A. (2002).** Motivational beliefs, values, and goals. *Annual Review of Psychology, 53*, 109–132. — Supports: expectancy–value framing behind the value and expectancy design guidance. Limits: a review of a large, heterogeneous literature.
- **Walton, G. M., & Cohen, G. L. (2011).** A brief social-belonging intervention improves academic and health outcomes of minority students. *Science, 331*(6023), 1447–1451. — Supports: belonging-uncertainty as a mechanism and the normalize-struggle framing. Limits: brief-intervention effects are context-dependent and have shown heterogeneous replication; treat as a mechanism to design for, not an effect to promise.
- **Harackiewicz, J. M., Canning, E. A., Tibbetts, Y., Priniski, S. J., & Hyde, J. S. (2016).** Closing achievement gaps with a utility-value intervention: Disentangling race and social class. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 111*(5), 745–765. — Supports: student-articulated relevance ("where would this matter" prompts) improving outcomes, with larger effects for first-generation and underrepresented students in biology. Limits: one course context; the mechanism, not the magnitude, transfers.
- **Canning, E. A., Muenks, K., Green, D. J., & Murphy, M. C. (2019).** STEM faculty who believe ability is fixed have larger racial achievement gaps and inspire less student motivation in their classes. *Science Advances, 5*(2). — Supports: instructor mindset as a course-climate variable. Limits: correlational at the instructor level.

## Teams and peer evaluation

- **Oakley, B., Felder, R. M., Brent, R., & Elhajj, I. (2004).** Turning student groups into effective teams. *Journal of Student Centered Learning, 2*(1), 9–34. — Supports: the team-formation, team-agreement, and dysfunction-handling practices in `references/evidence-informed-design.md` §10, including not isolating a lone underrepresented student. Limits: practice guidance grounded in experience and cited research, not a controlled study.
- **Ohland, M. W., Loughry, M. L., Woehr, D. J., Bullard, L. G., Felder, R. M., Finelli, C. J., Layton, R. A., Pomeranz, H. R., & Schmucker, D. G. (2012).** The Comprehensive Assessment of Team Member Effectiveness: Development of a behaviorally anchored rating scale for self- and peer evaluation. *Academy of Management Learning & Education, 11*(4), 609–630. — Supports: behaviorally anchored peer evaluation and the observable-behavior rule; the validation model for peer-evaluation instruments. Limits: validated for its populations and use; a locally modified version does not inherit the claim.
- **Springer, L., Stanne, M. E., & Donovan, S. S. (1999).** Effects of small-group learning on undergraduates in science, mathematics, engineering, and technology: A meta-analysis. *Review of Educational Research, 69*(1), 21–51. — Supports: meta-analytic evidence for small-group learning effects on achievement, persistence, and attitudes in SMET. Limits: aggregates diverse group formats.

## Feedback, assessment, and grading

- **Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998).** Assessment and classroom learning. *Assessment in Education: Principles, Policy & Practice, 5*(1), 7–74. — Supports: formative assessment's effect on learning; the feedback-then-use requirement. Limits: broad review across school levels; effect sizes debated, direction robust.
- **Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007).** The power of feedback. *Review of Educational Research, 77*(1), 81–112. — Supports: the task/process/regulation/self feedback distinction behind attributional-feedback guidance. Limits: meta-synthesis; praise-level feedback findings are the fragile part.
- **Carless, D., & Boud, D. (2018).** The development of student feedback literacy: Enabling uptake of feedback. *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 43*(8), 1315–1325. — Supports: designing for feedback uptake, not just delivery. Limits: conceptual framework.
- **Nilson, L. B. (2015).** *Specifications Grading: Restoring Rigor, Motivating Students, and Saving Faculty Time.* Stylus. — Supports: the specifications-grading scheme referenced in `references/assessment-quality.md` §8. Limits: practice-based case evidence, not controlled comparison.
- **Clark, D., & Talbert, R. (2023).** *Grading for Growth: A Guide to Alternative Grading Practices that Promote Authentic Learning and Student Engagement in Higher Education.* Stylus. — Supports: current survey of standards-based, specifications, and ungrading practices with implementation guidance. Limits: practice-based; evidence base for alternative grading remains promising rather than strong.
- **American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, & National Council on Measurement in Education (2014).** *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing.* AERA. — Supports: the authoritative validity, reliability, and fairness framework behind `references/assessment-quality.md` — validity as support for a specific interpretation and use. Limits: professional standards, not a how-to.
- **Winkelmes, M.-A., Bernacki, M., Butler, J., Zochowski, M., Golanics, J., & Weavil, K. H. (2016).** A teaching intervention that increases underserved college students' success. *Peer Review, 18*(1/2). — Supports: transparent purpose–task–criteria assignment design, with larger benefits for underserved students. Limits: quasi-experimental multi-institution study.
- **Haladyna, T. M., Downing, S. M., & Rodriguez, M. C. (2002).** A review of multiple-choice item-writing guidelines for classroom assessment. *Applied Measurement in Education, 15*(3), 309–334. — Supports: the selected-response item-writing rules in `references/artifact-patterns.md` §8, each graded by its research support. Limits: guideline evidence is uneven; the review itself reports which rules rest on studies and which on expert consensus.

## Named-format anchors

- **Crouch, C. H., & Mazur, E. (2001).** Peer Instruction: Ten years of experience and results. *American Journal of Physics, 69*(9), 970–977. — Supports: the peer-instruction row of the crosswalk, including the individual-commitment-first mechanism. Limits: physics courses at one institution over a decade.
- **Novak, G. M., Patterson, E. T., Gavrin, A. D., & Christian, W. (1999).** *Just-in-Time Teaching: Blending Active Learning with Web Technology.* Prentice Hall. — Supports: the JiTT design in the crosswalk. Limits: foundational description; technology specifics are dated, the mechanism is not.
- **Kapur, M. (2016).** Examining productive failure, productive success, unproductive failure, and unproductive success in learning. *Educational Psychologist, 51*(2), 289–299. — Supports: the productive-failure row, including why the consolidation phase is not optional. Limits: strongest in well-structured mathematics and science domains.
- **Moog, R. S., & Spencer, J. N. (Eds.) (2008).** *Process Oriented Guided Inquiry Learning (POGIL).* ACS Symposium Series 994. American Chemical Society. — Supports: the POGIL row — structured team processing of models with rotating roles, and the chemistry evidence base. Limits: an edited volume; individual chapters carry the studies.
- **Beichner, R. J., Saul, J. M., Abbott, D. S., Morse, J. J., Deardorff, D. L., Allain, R. J., Bonham, S. W., Dancy, M. H., & Risley, J. S. (2007).** The Student-Centered Activities for Large Enrollment Undergraduate Programs (SCALE-UP) project. In E. F. Redish & P. J. Cooney (Eds.), *Research-Based Reform of University Physics* (Reviews in PER, Vol. 1). American Association of Physics Teachers. — Supports: the SCALE-UP/studio row, including the room and staffing dependence the crosswalk flags as its fidelity failure. Limits: physics-centered evidence, largely from the originating institutions.
- **Zawojewski, J. S., Diefes-Dux, H. A., & Bowman, K. J. (Eds.) (2008).** *Models and Modeling in Engineering Education: Designing Experiences for All Students.* Sense Publishers. — Supports: the model-eliciting-activities row — client-driven tasks whose deliverable is a generalizable, documented model. Limits: design framework and case evidence rather than controlled comparison.
- **CAST (2018).** *Universal Design for Learning Guidelines version 2.2.* — Supports: the canonical statement of the UDL principles mapped in `references/framework-crosswalk.md` §3. Limits: guidelines, not an effect-size literature; check the current version at time of use.

## Delivery mode and workload

- **Bernard, R. M., Abrami, P. C., Lou, Y., Borokhovski, E., Wade, A., Wozney, L., Wallet, P. A., Fiset, M., & Huang, B. (2004).** How does distance education compare with classroom instruction? A meta-analysis of the empirical literature. *Review of Educational Research, 74*(3), 379–439. — Supports: the delivery-mode guidance's premise that modality itself explains little while design quality explains much — outcomes vary enormously within each mode. Limits: pre-modern-LMS studies; use for the design-over-modality principle, not for current platform comparisons.
- **Barre, E., & Esarey, J. (2016).** Course Workload Estimator. Rice University Center for Teaching Excellence. — Supports: the rate-based workload heuristics in `references/course-coherence-and-implementation.md` §3 — reading rates by density, difficulty, and purpose; writing rates by genre. Limits: a practice tool with documented research rationale, not a validated instrument; its rates are defaults to calibrate against local evidence.
