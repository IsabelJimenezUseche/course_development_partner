## 1. Use evidence proportionately

Do not call a recommendation “evidence-based” without identifying the evidence and its limits. Match the effort to the consequence of the decision.

Apply that standard to this reference too. The guidance in the sections below is synthesized practice guidance, not a citation. Its support is uneven: some patterns rest on large, repeatedly replicated bodies of work, others on smaller or more context-bound literatures, and the effect of any of them depends on the population, discipline, prior knowledge, and implementation conditions. Reading a rule here does not discharge the obligation to identify evidence for a consequential recommendation — record it in `assets/source-register.md` with the population it describes. Where a section states a direction of effect, treat that as a mechanism to check locally rather than a magnitude to promise.

Use this authority order:

1. professor-confirmed educational intent and disciplinary judgment;
2. applicable institutional requirements, accessibility guidance, and policy;
3. applicable program, professional, accreditation, code, or standard requirements;
4. discipline-based education research and relevant learning research;
5. credible practice guidance with a clear context;
6. illustrative or generated ideas, labeled as provisional.

Apply `references/evidence-source-protocol.md` and use `assets/source-register.md` for consequential recommendations. Record:

- the design claim;
- the source or professor-approved rationale;
- the population, discipline, modality, and conditions to which it applies;
- whether support is strong, promising, context-dependent, contested, or unknown;
- the implementation cost and plausible downside;
- what evidence after teaching would justify keeping or revising it.

Do not let a general pedagogy source override an authoritative course requirement. Present a decision card when evidence and a fixed requirement conflict.

Record copyright, license, attribution, adaptation, and reuse restrictions for source materials, examples, datasets, visuals, and student-created work. Do not assume that educational use permits redistribution.

## 2. Select a learning mechanism

Choose an activity because of the cognitive, social, or affective work it enables. State the mechanism before naming the format.

Use this sequence:

1. Name the target change in what students can notice, explain, choose, produce, or judge.
2. Identify the current knowledge, model, strategy, or barrier that affects that change.
3. Select a mechanism that can produce observable evidence of the change.
4. Choose an activity format that makes students perform that work.
5. Add feedback and a later opportunity to use it.
6. Define what would show that the activity was ineffective.

Reject an engaging activity when students can complete it without the intended reasoning. Do not treat participation, enjoyment, or completion as evidence of learning.

## 3. Establish what students already know

The conceptual-change cycle, scaffold depth, and cognitive-load decisions all depend on the current student model. Establish it from evidence; do not assert it from intuition or from what the syllabus says students should have retained.

Use the cheapest source that answers the question:

1. **An existing validated instrument.** Most STEM disciplines have published concept inventories and diagnostic instruments — force and motion, electricity and magnetism, statics, thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, signals and systems, chemical bonding and equilibrium, genetics, natural selection, statistical reasoning, and introductory computing among them. Before writing a diagnostic from scratch, check whether a validated instrument covers the concept. Ask the instructor whether one is already used in the course or program.
2. **Discipline-based education research on the specific concept.** Documented misconception catalogs exist for many core STEM topics and are more reliable than generated guesses. Apply `references/evidence-source-protocol.md` and record what you find in `assets/source-register.md`.
3. **The instructor's own observation.** Prior-offering exam responses, recurring office-hour questions, and known failure points are legitimate, high-fit evidence. Ask for them directly.
4. **A short purpose-built diagnostic.** Use when no instrument or record exists. Ask for a prediction, explanation, sketch, ranking, or classification that distinguishes competing models — not a recall quiz.

Observe these limits:

- A published instrument is validated for a stated population, level, and use. Do not assume transfer to a different level, modality, or translated version; do not modify items and retain the validation claim.
- Many instruments are secured by their authors. Do not reproduce items, generate imitations of secured items, or place them in student-facing material. Record access and reuse restrictions in the source register.
- Do not use a diagnostic that will be reused as a post-measure for graded practice in between.
- A concept inventory measures a specific construct. It is not a general placement test, a proxy for course grade, or evidence about an individual student's ability.

Report the current model as a claim with a source: *observed in this course*, *documented in the literature for this population*, or *provisional and untested*. When it is provisional, say what would confirm it.

## 4. Design for conceptual change

Use this cycle when students hold a stable but incomplete or incorrect mental model:

1. **Elicit:** ask for a prediction, explanation, sketch, classification, or decision that exposes the current model.
2. **Make evidence visible:** present an observation, case, result, or comparison that the current model must explain.
3. **Examine the discrepancy:** ask students to identify what the model explains, where it fails, and which assumptions caused the failure.
4. **Reconstruct:** support students in building or selecting a more adequate model, including its scope and limits.
5. **Re-test:** use a new case that cannot be solved by copying the earlier response.
6. **Reflect:** ask what changed in the model and which evidence justified the change.

Do not simply announce that a belief is a misconception. Avoid a trick demonstration whose result students remember without revising the underlying model.

Preview the cycle as:

```markdown
Target model:
Likely current model:
Elicitation evidence:
Discrepant or contrasting evidence:
Reconstruction support:
New case for re-test:
Evidence of revised understanding:
```

### Recognize threshold concepts and expert blind spots

Not every stubborn bottleneck is an ordinary misconception, and misdiagnosing the kind of difficulty wastes the design.

A **threshold concept** is transformative and integrative — entropy, reactive power, dynamic equilibrium, recursion, statistical inference as reasoning under uncertainty — and crossing it changes how students see the rest of the subject. Expect these differences from ordinary misconceptions: students occupy a prolonged in-between state in which they can execute procedures while the underlying idea remains troublesome; a single elicit-confront-resolve cycle rarely completes the change; and regression under load is normal, not evidence the teaching failed. Design for a threshold by distributing repeated returns to the concept across the curriculum map, in varied representations, with the in-between state treated as expected and named for students — rather than by scheduling one confrontation activity and recording the concept as resolved.

A related failure sits with the expert, not the student: instructors reliably underestimate difficulty at the steps they themselves automated long ago — where they select a model, choose a coordinate system, decide what to neglect, or read a representation without noticing they are decoding it. When the instructor reports that students "just can't do" a task, walk the task with them step by step and ask at each step what an expert is deciding and what evidence a student would need to make the same decision. The bottleneck usually sits at a step the instructor did not think to teach because they no longer experience it as a step. Record the decomposed steps as the scaffold plan; they are the supports that later fade.

## 5. Use high-value learning patterns

Use only the patterns that serve the objective.

### Retrieval

Ask students to retrieve prerequisite knowledge before re-explaining it. Keep retrieval low-stakes when its purpose is practice. Follow it with corrective feedback and another opportunity to retrieve or apply the knowledge.

### Distributed practice

Spread practice on an outcome across the sequence instead of concentrating it in the module where the topic is introduced. Return to the outcome after a gap, in a later module, embedded in new work rather than as a review block. Distribution is a scheduling decision, so make it in the curriculum map: an outcome whose practice all sits in one module is massed regardless of how many exercises it contains.

Set the spacing from when the outcome is next needed. Short gaps suit an outcome that a following module depends on immediately; longer gaps suit an outcome that must survive to a final assessment or a later course.

### Interleaving

When students must learn to *select* among methods, models, or representations, mix problem types within a practice set rather than blocking them by type. Blocked practice lets students infer the method from the section heading, which trains execution while hiding the selection decision that the outcome usually names.

Interleave only across genuinely confusable alternatives, and only after students can execute each method individually. Interleaving unrelated topics adds load without improving discrimination.

### Warn the instructor about the performance cost

Distributed and interleaved practice reliably depress performance and fluency *during* practice while improving later retention and transfer. Students and instructors read that slowdown as a defect. When recommending either pattern, state the tradeoff explicitly, and recommend telling students why the practice feels harder. Do not introduce either pattern silently into a graded activity where the dip will be scored.

### Worked and contrasting examples

Model expert decisions as well as final steps. Make selection cues, assumptions, checks, and interpretation visible. Compare examples that differ on one consequential feature. Gradually remove steps or prompts as students gain competence.

### Self-explanation

Ask students why a step, representation, model, or criterion applies. Avoid generic “explain your answer” prompts; name the relationship or choice students should justify.

### Feedback and use

Make feedback specific to the objective, timely enough to use, and actionable. Include a revision, retry, comparison, or planning step so students do something with feedback.

### Scaffold fading

State which support is temporary. Fade hints, decomposed steps, formulas, decision cues, or templates before independent performance is assessed. Do not remove supports unrelated to the target construct.

### Transfer

Change a meaningful feature of the context, representation, constraint, or available evidence. Ask students to identify what transfers, what changes, and why. Do not label a near-copy as transfer.

## 6. Diagnose cognitive load

Inspect the task from a student perspective. Separate difficulty inherent to the objective from avoidable load introduced by the material.

Check:

- unfamiliar notation, symbols, vocabulary, or units introduced simultaneously;
- directions split across pages, slides, systems, or oral repair;
- representations that must be mentally integrated;
- decorative information competing with relevant evidence;
- unnecessary tool switching, navigation, copying, or transcription;
- too many interacting decisions before students have a usable schema;
- memory demands unrelated to the intended outcome;
- worked examples that hide expert decisions or skip intermediate reasoning.

Reduce avoidable load by sequencing elements, integrating related information, signaling critical features, providing accessible reference support, and postponing secondary complexity. Preserve productive difficulty required by the objective.

## 7. Design for learner variability and inclusion

Address barriers during intake and sequence design, not only during final accessibility review.

Ask about requirements and constraints without requesting unnecessary diagnoses, accommodation details, demographics, or personal histories. Distinguish:

- **universal design choice:** improves access or participation for many students;
- **individual accommodation:** follows an authoritative approved process;
- **construct-preserving option:** changes access or expression without changing the learning target;
- **construct-changing option:** changes what is being learned or assessed and requires instructor approval.

When the objective permits, provide multiple means of:

- engagement: relevance, meaningful choice, transparent purpose, and supported participation;
- representation: text, visuals, examples, notation support, and accessible alternatives;
- action or expression: accessible response modes and tools that preserve the construct.

Make goals, success criteria, participation expectations, and help pathways explicit. Inspect examples, contexts, language, symbols, group structures, and response modes for exclusionary assumptions, stereotypes, identity threats, or barriers to belonging.

Record which barriers were removed, which remain, and which require accessibility or institutional expertise.

## 8. Support motivation, self-efficacy, and belonging

Whether students engage the cognitive design at all depends on whether they expect to succeed and whether the work seems worth doing. Persistence in gateway and high-attrition STEM courses is not explained by content difficulty alone, and the affective load is not evenly distributed: students without family or peer experience in the field, and students who are underrepresented in the discipline, have fewer external signals to weigh against the inference that ordinary struggle means they do not belong. Treat that as a design consideration to check, not a measured property of any particular cohort.

Treat this as design work with the same discipline used elsewhere: name the mechanism, keep the claim bounded, and do not promise attitude change from a single activity.

### Value

Make the reason for the work available to students rather than only to the instructor. The context brief already records why a context improves the learning task — express the student-facing half of that in the artifact.

- Connect the task to a use students recognize: a downstream course, professional practice, a decision that affects people, or a question in the discipline.
- Prefer having students articulate the connection over asserting it for them; a short "where would this matter" prompt makes the student do the connecting, which a paragraph of motivation text does not.
- Do not manufacture relevance. A fabricated application that students recognize as fake costs credibility.

### Expectancy and self-efficacy

- Sequence an early, genuine success on the target construct — not a trivial task, one that is attainable with the support provided.
- Give attributional feedback that points to strategy and effort under the student's control ("this applied the model outside the range where its assumptions hold") rather than to fixed ability ("this is a hard concept for most people").
- Make success criteria and the standard of good work visible in advance; hidden standards penalize students who lack access to informal channels.
- Normalize productive struggle explicitly, especially where the design deliberately introduces desirable difficulty. Tell students that the work is supposed to feel effortful and that difficulty is not a verdict.
- Show that the discipline contains people who resemble the students in it, through examples, contexts, and cited work, without singling any student out as a representative.

### Autonomy and participation

- Offer meaningful choice — of context, dataset, problem, or medium — where it does not change the construct. Record such a choice as a construct-preserving option under §7.
- State participation expectations and help pathways plainly, and make help-seeking a normal step in the process rather than a signal of deficit.

### Limits

Do not claim a motivational or affective effect from an uncontrolled classroom observation, and do not report satisfaction as evidence of learning. Do not collect psychological, demographic, or attitudinal data to support these choices without the governance path in `references/course-coherence-and-implementation.md`; the design choices above are defensible without measuring students. Keep the mechanism in the design log, not in student-facing text where it reads as manipulation.

## 9. Support metacognition

When appropriate, ask students to:

- plan an approach and name the evidence they need;
- monitor progress against a criterion;
- compare strategies or representations;
- explain how feedback changed the next attempt;
- identify what will transfer to a new problem;
- reflect on uncertainty and limits without substituting reflection for performance.

## 10. Design collaborative and team learning

Design teamwork rather than assuming it. Collaboration that is only a division of labor produces a submitted artifact and little individual learning, and it is the most common way group work fails in STEM courses.

### Justify and structure the collaboration

Define:

- the reason collaboration improves this learning task — division of effort is not one;
- interdependent work that cannot be completed by splitting independent pieces and stapling them together;
- individual preparation required before the team meets;
- equitable participation structures and meaningful roles;
- team-process feedback and a conflict or help pathway;
- evidence of both individual and collective learning.

Do not assign roles that repeatedly place the same students in clerical or low-status work. Rotate roles that carry visible technical authority.

### Form teams deliberately

Team composition is a design decision, not an administrative one.

- State the formation basis — instructor-assigned, self-selected, or criterion-based — and the reason for it. Self-selection is fastest and tends to reproduce existing social groupings and isolate students who lack them.
- Distribute relevant prior experience across teams rather than concentrating it, so that no team depends on one member and no team lacks a foothold.
- Avoid placing a single student from an underrepresented group alone on a team when the cohort makes an alternative possible and an institution-authorized process supplies the relevant information. Never infer identity from names, appearance, or records collected for another purpose, and do not request sensitive identity data solely to form teams. When authorized information is unavailable, improve role rotation, participation structures, monitoring, and escalation pathways for every team rather than identity-matching students.
- Consider schedule, modality, and access constraints, which determine whether a team can meet at all. Ask about availability rather than about personal circumstances.
- Set team size from the task: large enough that the work genuinely requires division, small enough that no member can be passive. Fix the duration and say whether teams persist across the term.

### Support the team as a working unit

- Require an early team agreement covering roles, meeting logistics, communication, decision-making, and what the team will do when a member does not deliver.
- Schedule at least one process checkpoint before the final deliverable, so that a failing team can be corrected rather than diagnosed afterward.
- Define the escalation path and what the instructor will and will not intervene in. Publish it in advance.
- Teach the collaboration skill being assessed if it is an outcome. Teamwork that is graded but never taught is assessed prior experience.

### Separate individual and team evidence

- Keep an individual-performance channel for every outcome that is claimed for individuals — individual components, an exam item, an oral check, or a documented individual contribution.
- Do not let a team product be the sole evidence of an individual outcome.

Peer evaluation, its instrument, and the way team and individual scores combine into a grade are assessment-design decisions. Apply `references/assessment-quality.md` before using peer input in a consequential grade.

## 11. Report the rationale

Use this compact record for consequential choices:

```markdown
Design choice:
Target learning change:
Learning mechanism:
Evidence or approved rationale:
Applicability and limits:
Accessibility and inclusion considerations:
Implementation cost or risk:
Evidence to collect after use:
Instructor decision required: yes | no
```

Keep this record in the design log. Do not expose internal evidence notes in student-facing material unless they help students understand the purpose or success criteria.
