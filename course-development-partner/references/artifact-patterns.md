## 1. Common contract

For every artifact, identify:

- audience and teaching use;
- mapped learning outcome and intended cognitive demand;
- prerequisites and likely misconceptions;
- delivery constraints;
- progressive preview;
- student-facing and instructor-facing components;
- required validation passes;
- source and provenance needs;
- editable and rendered output requirements.

Keep related artifacts synchronized. Never allow the solution, rubric, or instructor guide to introduce an unstated requirement or assumption.

## 2. Lesson plans

Preview: alignment map and time-block outline.

Include:

- observable outcomes;
- prerequisites and preparation;
- time blocks;
- instructor and student actions;
- checks for understanding;
- transition language;
- debrief and closure prompts;
- materials and technology;
- instructor/TA guidance;
- contingency options.

Validate that the plan is feasible for the stated class size, room, time, staffing, and technology.

## 3. Active-learning worksheets

Preview: ordered reasoning/task sequence with the purpose of each step.

Design a progression toward meaningful challenge. Include visible progress markers, adequate workspace, group roles when useful, checkpoints, synthesis, and debrief. Avoid busywork and disconnected question sets.

Create:

- student worksheet;
- instructor facilitation guide;
- solution or exemplar;
- feedback or scoring guide when appropriate.

Verify that directions are usable without excessive oral repair and that the key uses only information and assumptions available to students.

## 4. Context-rich problems

Preview: context brief.

Connect a credible setting, stakeholder, disciplinary decision, concept or model, evidence, assumptions, and interpretation. Make context affect student reasoning rather than only renaming objects in a textbook exercise.

When appropriate, ask students to:

- select and justify a model or method;
- state assumptions;
- track units and dimensions;
- interpret a result;
- examine limitations or sensitivity;
- recommend an action.

Remove irrelevant detail unless distinguishing signal from noise is an explicit objective. Independently verify facts, data, equations, and solvability.

## 5. Assessments

Preview: assessment blueprint with outcome, cognitive demand, item type, points, and time.

Map every item to an outcome. Check:

- solvability and ambiguity;
- cognitive-demand match;
- time and point balance;
- accessibility and accommodations;
- information leakage;
- independence or intentional dependency among items;
- fairness across valid methods;
- technical correctness.

Independently solve quantitative items before use. Distinguish diagnostic, formative, and summative purposes.

## 6. Rubrics and calibration

### Clarify rubric intent

Ask the instructor:

1. Which educational objectives and evidence should the rubric evaluate?
2. Is the use formative, summative, or both?
3. Should the structure be analytic, holistic, single-point, checklist, or another form?
4. What scale, thresholds, and required weights apply?
5. Which scoring orientation should govern?
6. How should conceptual, procedural, computational, communicative, and minor errors be treated?
7. Should an early error propagate, be penalized once, or receive carry-forward credit?
8. How should partial reasoning and alternative correct methods be treated?

Explain the orientations:

- **Objective-achievement-based:** award credit for evidence that the objective was achieved. Favor it when reasoning, interpretation, transfer, design judgment, or multiple valid approaches matter.
- **Error/deduction-based:** begin with full credit and subtract defined errors. Use it when correctness requirements are discrete and stable; prevent accumulated deductions from distorting the construct.
- **Balanced — recommended:** anchor criteria and weights in the objectives, then treat errors as evidence of the degree of achievement. Scale consequences to how much an error undermines the target learning.

Ask the instructor to choose an orientation before producing a final rubric. Do not infer the choice from an existing rubric or grading pattern. Default to balanced and objective-first only when the instructor delegates the choice. Do not turn the rubric into a punishment list detached from learning.

### Build the initial rubric

Preview criteria, weights, scale, and boundary logic before drafting full descriptors.

Then:

1. Derive criteria from observable evidence.
2. Weight criteria by objective importance.
3. Write observable performance distinctions.
4. Define partial-credit and alternative-method logic.
5. Define error dependency, carry-forward, and maximum-deduction rules.
6. Check for double penalties and irrelevant surface criteria.

### Request de-identified student responses

After the initial rubric exists, ask for a small authentic set. Suggest, but do not require, examples representing:

- strong work;
- typical or middle-range work;
- an important score or grade boundary;
- weak work;
- an unexpected but potentially valid approach;
- a recurring response that exposed ambiguity.

Request removal of names, identifiers, existing grades, accommodation information, and unrelated personal details. Do not process identifiable examples without an approved privacy path.

### Calibrate interactively

1. Obtain the de-identified response content, or ask the instructor to confirm that supplied summaries contain enough evidence for calibration.
2. Ask what each response demonstrates about the educational objective before recommending or revealing a score.
3. Apply the current rubric consistently only after the instructor provides that judgment.
4. Compare results with instructor judgments.
5. Diagnose disagreement as a criterion, weight, descriptor, valid-method, error-dependency, boundary, or expectation issue.
6. Propose focused revisions and show their effect on example scores.
7. Ask the instructor to approve consequential changes.
8. Re-score every example after revision.
9. Check whether another grader could apply the interpretation consistently.

Create an instructor/grader calibration guide containing de-identified boundary examples, approved interpretations, dependency rules, valid alternatives, and common misconceptions. Keep it separate from the student-facing rubric.

### Validate the rubric

Confirm:

- criteria and weights primarily measure the stated objectives;
- descriptors use observable evidence;
- partial understanding receives defensible credit;
- conceptual errors differ from arithmetic, transcription, formatting, and communication errors;
- one underlying mistake is not penalized repeatedly without explicit justification;
- later correct reasoning receives carry-forward credit where appropriate;
- alternative correct methods can receive full credit;
- examples produce defensible scores and feedback;
- student expectations are clear without revealing answers or constraining valid approaches.

## 7. Solution keys

Include:

- assumptions and givens;
- expected reasoning and intermediate steps;
- units, dimensions, equations, and checks;
- interpretation;
- alternative valid approaches;
- common errors;
- grading notes and dependency rules.

Verify the solution independently from the generated prompt. Do not silently repair an unsolvable or ambiguous student item only in the key.

## 8. Slides and visuals

Preview a storyboard or visual specification.

Use visuals for relationships, mechanisms, comparisons, hierarchy, or change over time. Check:

- disciplinary accuracy;
- labels, scales, legends, and reading order;
- contrast and legibility;
- textual alternatives;
- whether meaning depends on color alone;
- whether decorative elements compete with learning.

Keep text, diagrams, and tables editable where the format permits. Render and inspect final slides or images.

## 9. Study guides

Organize around what students should be able to do, not merely topic names. Include:

- objective-linked skills;
- prerequisite reminders;
- misconceptions;
- representative problem or question types;
- decision cues for selecting a concept or method;
- self-check questions;
- study strategies and help pathways.

Do not reproduce an exam or disclose protected answers.

## 10. Communications, surveys, and media

### Student communications

Make purpose, required action, deadline, criteria, and help pathway easy to find. Use respectful, direct language and explain administrative terms.

### Surveys and reflections

Map each item to a decision or research question. Avoid collecting unnecessary identifying data. Distinguish course improvement from human-subjects research and follow institutional requirements.

### Audio and video

Preview a script and storyboard. Include captions or transcript, pronunciation guidance, pacing, visual descriptions, sources, and accessibility alternatives. Validate disciplinary content and rendered media before delivery.
