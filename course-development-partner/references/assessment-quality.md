## 1. Scale rigor to consequences

Ask what decision will be made from the assessment and who may be affected. Increase review, piloting, scorer preparation, security, and documentation as consequences increase.

Distinguish these evidence levels:

- **Classroom quality review:** alignment, clarity, solvability, accessibility, timing, and scoring logic were checked.
- **Expert review:** qualified reviewers examined content, construct representation, and intended use.
- **Pilot evidence:** representative students or responses revealed timing, interpretation, difficulty, or scoring issues.
- **Reliability evidence:** consistency was examined across raters, occasions, forms, or items as appropriate.
- **Formal validation:** multiple sources of evidence support a specified interpretation and use for a defined population and context.

Do not call an assessment or rubric “validated” when only a classroom review or a few calibration examples exist. State exactly what was examined.

## 2. Build an assessment blueprint

Use `assets/assessment-blueprint.md` before drafting a consequential assessment.

For each item, record:

- mapped outcome and intended cognitive demand;
- intended interpretation or use;
- observable evidence claim;
- item type, points, and expected time;
- dependencies on other items, resources, tools, or prior errors;
- likely construct-irrelevant barriers;
- accessibility and accommodation considerations;
- permitted collaboration, resources, and AI use;
- review status.

Inspect the blueprint for:

- outcomes not sampled or sampled too narrowly;
- excessive weighting on easily scored but lower-priority knowledge;
- cognitive demand below or above the outcome;
- item dependencies that amplify one error;
- time or reading load that distorts the target construct;
- assessment density inconsistent with available practice and feedback.

## 3. State a validity argument

For any consequential score, answer:

1. What interpretation or decision will be made?
2. What student evidence should support that interpretation?
3. How does the task elicit that evidence?
4. Which important parts of the outcome are not sampled?
5. What alternative explanations could produce a high or low score?
6. What evidence supports the scoring rules?
7. For whom and under which conditions is the interpretation intended?
8. What consequences or misuse must be prevented?

Treat validity as support for a particular interpretation and use, not as a permanent property of an item. Do not generalize evidence from one course, population, modality, or scoring process without justification.

## 4. Protect fairness and accessibility

Check for construct-irrelevant variance from:

- language, cultural knowledge, notation, or context not required by the objective;
- inaccessible media, navigation, response mode, or timing;
- tool access, device performance, bandwidth, or prior exposure unrelated to the objective;
- stereotypes, identity threats, or assumptions about students' experiences;
- administration differences or inconsistent help;
- rubric criteria that reward polish, format, or conformity beyond the outcome.

Distinguish construct-preserving access from a change to the construct. Follow authoritative accommodation processes. Do not request unnecessary disability or personal information.

Recognize multiple valid approaches. Define how alternative methods, partial reasoning, error propagation, and carry-forward credit affect interpretation. Prevent one underlying error from generating multiple penalties unless the instructor explicitly justifies the rule.

## 5. Prepare and calibrate scorers

Scale scorer preparation to the stakes, judgment required, number of graders, and available resources.

For consequential rubric use:

1. Recommend four purposeful de-identified examples—strong, boundary or middle-range, weak, and alternative-method or recurring-ambiguity—when available. For a small course or limited evidence, accept two available examples only with explicit provisional status and documented coverage limitations.
2. Have at least two qualified graders score independently when feasible.
3. Compare criterion-level judgments before comparing only total scores.
4. Classify disagreements as criterion, descriptor, evidence, weight, dependency, valid-method, boundary, or expectation issues.
5. Revise the rubric, examples, or scorer guidance rather than forcing agreement without diagnosis.
6. Re-score the sample after revision.
7. Record unresolved disagreements and the moderation or escalation rule.
8. State when no consistency evidence is available.

Do not let AI serve as the second qualified grader for consequential use. AI may organize discrepancies or apply provisional rules, but a responsible human must approve interpretations and decisions.

Choose scoring logic from the construct and consequences rather than a universal default. Use objective-achievement or balanced logic when evidence can be partial, multidimensional, reasoned, or legitimately diverse. Use checklist/mastery or explicit error rules when verified discrete requirements, safety steps, or thresholds govern. Document any combined logic and prevent double penalties.

## 6. Design for the permitted-AI context

Ask the instructor which AI uses are permitted, restricted, or required by institutional or course policy. Do not invent policy.

Prefer assessment evidence aligned to the objective, such as:

- documented reasoning and decision points;
- staged drafts and feedback use;
- oral explanation or defense when feasible and equitable;
- application to local, novel, or changing evidence;
- comparison and critique of generated or alternative approaches;
- reflection tied to observable work rather than unsupported self-report.

Do not claim an item is “AI-proof.” Do not default to surveillance, hidden traps, or unnecessary complexity. Check whether an AI restriction changes the construct, creates unequal access, or adds workload unrelated to the objective.

## 7. Apply high-stakes release gates

Before releasing a high-stakes assessment, confirm:

- applicable policy and authoritative requirements;
- content accuracy and independent solution verification;
- outcome coverage and cognitive-demand match;
- administration conditions, timing, and permitted resources;
- accessibility and approved accommodations;
- security, version control, and information-leakage controls;
- scoring guide, scorer preparation, and moderation;
- alternative-method and dependency rules;
- student communication and transparent success criteria;
- regrade, appeal, exception, and incident pathways;
- instructor approval of unresolved limitations.

Treat a failure involving safety, solvability, material unfairness, inaccessible required use, or unsupported consequential interpretation as a blocker.

## 8. Report claims and limitations

Use this record:

```markdown
Assessment purpose:
Intended interpretation or decision:
Population and conditions:
Evidence reviewed:
Outcome coverage and gaps:
Fairness and accessibility review:
Scorer preparation and consistency evidence:
Permitted-AI context:
Known limitations:
Appropriate claim: classroom-reviewed | expert-reviewed | piloted | reliability examined | formally validated
Instructor approval:
```

Keep sensitive assessment content and student examples in approved locations. Keep de-identified calibration examples separate from the student-facing rubric.
